A twenty-minute class with Earshot.

Students who are blind, have low vision or see well can do every step together, by ear. The right answers come from NASA's data and are shown on the page, so you do not need to be an expert. For ages ten and up.

You need: Earshot open in a browser, speakers or headphones (headphones make left and right clearer), and ideally a globe or a raised-line world map.

1. How a map can sound

3 minutes

Say: "NASA watches our planet from space and shows what it sees as pictures. Today we will listen to those pictures instead."

Explain the three rules: low notes are the south of the planet and high notes the north; the sound travels from west to east, from your left ear to your right; louder means more. Then press "Hear the eight voices" in "How it works" so everyone hears the notes climb from south to north.

2. Where is the ocean warm?

4 minutes

Choose "Sea temperature" and press "Play this record". Ask everyone to point, or say, which voices were loudest: the high notes, the low notes or the middle ones.

Answer: the middle voices, around the equator, where the sea is warmest. A hiss means land, where this map has no sea. Press "Read it aloud" to hear the briefing confirm it.

3. How has the world changed?

5 minutes

Choose "Night lights". Press "Play all 12 in order" to hear every year from 2012 to 2023. Then press "Hear only what changed": now only the places where light grew make a sound.

Ask: "Where did the most light appear?" Earshot then says it in words. Discuss: what makes a place brighter at night? Growing towns, new roads, more homes with electricity.

4. What is around us today?

4 minutes

Choose "Haze" or "Smoke and dust". Type your own town in "Ask about one place" and press "Hear it", then press "Hear the area around" your town. A soft chime rings as the sound passes your town.

Ask: "Is there more to the north or the south of us?" Earshot then says how far away the most is, and in which direction.

5. Test your ears

4 minutes

Everyone closes their eyes. Press "Start the check" in "Can you hear the map?". Three short maps play, and the class answers each question together. At the end, compare the class's score with what guessing would score.

To take it further

  • Students write down a measurement for several cities, such as today's temperature, as a table with the columns lat, lon and value, and drop it into "Bring your own map" to hear their own data.
  • Download a map's sound, send it to a classmate, and have them drop it back into Earshot: the map comes back from the sound alone.
  • Subscribe to the daily feed for your town in a podcast app and listen each morning for a week. What changes?

For students who are blind or have low vision

  • Every control works from the keyboard. On the world map, the arrow keys move and Enter speaks the place and the value.
  • If a student uses a screen reader, leave the page voice off: the screen reader reads every answer.
  • "One band at a time" in the Voices setting plays each part of the planet on its own, which is easier for a first listen.
  • Each picture is described in words under "What the picture looks like".
